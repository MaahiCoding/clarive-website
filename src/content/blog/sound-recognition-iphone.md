Sound Recognition makes your iPhone listen for sounds you'd rather not miss and notify you when it hears one. Apple's examples are a doorbell, a siren and a crying baby. It covers sound you can't hear or aren't listening for, and it's limited, because all it does is name a sound.

This guide covers how to turn it on in iOS 26 and later, where the menu moved, how to teach it your own doorbell or appliance, and the limits the how-to pages skip. It's one of several hearing tools already on your phone, and our [iPhone hearing tools](/blog/topics/iphone-hearing/) guides cover the rest.

## What Sound Recognition does

Your iPhone can [continuously listen](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-sound-recognition-iphf2dc33312/ios) for the sounds you pick and notify you when it recognizes one. Apple's [hearing accessibility page](https://www.apple.com/accessibility/hearing/) says it recognizes 15 different sounds and uses on-device intelligence to do it. You can also train it on your own name, and on electronic sounds specific to your home: appliance beeps, a particular alarm, a doorbell. It works in CarPlay too, where Apple says the phone listens for sirens, horns and a crying baby and shows the notification on the car's screen.

Here's the situation it's built for. You're at the kitchen table with headphones on and the phone beside you, and someone rings the doorbell. Your ears miss it. The phone's microphone doesn't, so you get a notification.

That works whether you're hard of hearing and worry about the doorbell, or you have typical hearing and a pair of noise-cancelling earbuds. The feature doesn't care why you missed the sound.

## How to turn on Sound Recognition

Apple moved this setting. In iOS 18 it was Settings, Accessibility, Sound Recognition. In iOS 26 and iOS 27 it sits under a new Sound & Name Recognition heading. I compared Apple's guides for both versions to be sure, and it's why AbilityNet's iOS 18 walkthrough and the older explainers send you to a path that doesn't match your screen. Here's the current one:

1. Open Settings, tap Accessibility, then tap Sound & Name Recognition.
2. Tap Sound Recognition and turn it on. If iPhone asks, wait while it downloads the sounds.
3. Tap Sounds and turn on the ones you want.
4. Optional: add the Sound Recognition control to Control Center (the plus in the top-left corner, then Add a Control) so you can switch it on and off in one tap.

Pick fewer sounds than you think you need. AbilityNet's guide notes that the more sounds you turn on, the more storage and memory it uses. Start with the two or three that matter in your home, like the doorbell, and add more when you miss something.

AbilityNet also reports that each sound can get its own alert tone, and that a sleeping iPhone wakes up to show the notification on the Lock Screen. If you use Hey Siri, expect a warning when you switch Sound Recognition on: AbilityNet's iOS 18 guide says Hey Siri stops working while it's running. Apple's current guide doesn't mention it, so read the prompt on your own phone.

## Teach it your own doorbell or appliance

The built-in list covers common sounds. Your washing machine's end-of-cycle chime isn't on it, and neither is the doorbell tune you picked in a smart-home app. For those, Apple lets you record your own.

Go back to Settings, Accessibility, Sound & Name Recognition, Sound Recognition, then Sounds. Tap Custom Alarm or Custom Appliance or Doorbell and give it a name. Apple's instruction is to place the iPhone near the sound and minimize background noise, then tap Start Listening and follow the prompts. Those two choices are the whole menu, so it's meant for alarms, appliances and doorbells and nothing else.

AbilityNet's walkthrough has you record the sound five times, then wait while the phone trains itself and sends a notification when it's done. Do it in a quiet room, with the real appliance running. A recording made with the TV on teaches the phone a sound that doesn't exist in your kitchen.

## What it can't do

This is the part the how-to pages skip.

**It isn't a safety device.** Apple's support page says it plainly: don't rely on your iPhone to recognize sounds where you may be harmed or injured, in high-risk or emergency situations, or for navigation. Keep your real smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. If you can't hear a standard alarm, the NIDCD describes [alerting devices](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/assistive-devices-people-hearing-voice-speech-or-language-disorders) that connect to a doorbell, telephone or alarm and use a loud sound or blinking light to signal an event. That's the category to look at for anything that matters.

**It only hears what the phone hears.** The microphone belongs to the phone. One charging in the bedroom won't catch a doorbell downstairs, so carry it with you or park it where you'll be.

**It names a sound. It doesn't read speech.** Sound Recognition can tell you the doorbell rang. It can't tell you what the person at the door said. The closest it gets to speech is your own name, which Apple lets you train it to listen for, and Apple says that part supports over 50 languages and locales.

**Apple doesn't say how well it works.** Its support page gives no accuracy figure and no detection distance, so there's nothing to hold it to. Treat a silent phone as no information, not as an all clear.

## Which tool for which problem

The iPhone has several features with "listen" in the name, and they solve different problems:

- **Sound Recognition** watches for non-speech sounds and alerts you. It works when you aren't paying attention to the room.
- **[Live Listen](/blog/live-listen-iphone/)** streams the phone's microphone to supported AirPods, Beats or hearing aids. Use it to hear a person across a room.
- **[Conversation Boost](/blog/conversation-boost/)** focuses AirPods Pro on the person facing you.
- **[Live captions](/blog/live-transcribe-iphone/)** write out what people say, so the words don't depend on your ears.

My position: turn Sound Recognition on first, even if you never touch the others. It's built into iOS and you can switch it off in seconds. It's the only one of these that helps when you aren't already in the conversation. Then pick the speech tool that matches your situation.

And if you keep missing sounds other people catch, like the doorbell, the phone or the microwave, that's worth a hearing test with an audiologist or your doctor, not a settings change.

## The short version

Sound Recognition is a free alert for the sounds you'd miss: a doorbell, a siren, a crying baby, plus your own alarms and appliances if you teach them. In iOS 26 and later it's under Sound & Name Recognition. Keep it away from anything safety-critical, and use something else for speech.
