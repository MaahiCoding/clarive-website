You add the Hearing control, tap Live Listen, and nothing reaches your headphones. Nothing's broken. Live Listen was never able to send audio to them.

## What Live Listen actually is

Live Listen turns your iPhone into a remote microphone. It is one of several [iPhone hearing tools](/blog/topics/iphone-hearing/) worth knowing about. Put the phone near whoever you're trying to hear, across a restaurant table or at the front of a lecture room, and its microphone picks up the sound and pipes it to your ears. It's genuinely useful, and it's been part of iOS for years.

The catch is the last step. Live Listen doesn't send audio out through the normal route that music and podcasts use. It uses a separate path Apple built for hearing accessibility, and only three kinds of device can receive it:

- AirPods (most models, including the standard ones)
- Beats headphones with Apple's H-series chip
- Hearing devices certified under the [Made for iPhone program](https://support.apple.com/en-us/HT201466)

Everything else is excluded. Not throttled, not degraded. Excluded. Your Sony over-ears, the Bluetooth earbuds that came free with something, the wired EarPods in the drawer: none of them can receive a Live Listen stream, and no setting will change that.

That surprises people, and I think reasonably so. The headphones work for every other sound the phone makes. It's a hardware permission problem wearing the costume of a bug.

## How to check whether yours will work

Faster than reading a compatibility list:

1. Open **Settings → Control Center** and add **Hearing** if it isn't there already.
2. Connect the headphones you want to use.
3. Open Control Center and tap the ear icon.
4. Tap **Live Listen**.

If your headphones are eligible, audio starts immediately and you'll hear the room get louder. If they aren't, Live Listen either won't offer them or will switch to your iPhone's speaker, which defeats the purpose fairly comprehensively.

Menu wording shifts a little between iOS versions, but the ear icon in Control Center is the constant.

## What works instead

The restriction is on Apple's private audio path, not on your iPhone's microphone. Any app can read the microphone and play the result back through the standard output, the same route your music takes. That route works with every set of headphones your phone can connect to.

That's the whole reason [third-party amplification apps](/) exist, and why they don't care what's on your head.

Two things are worth knowing before you try one:

**Connection type changes the experience more than the app does.** Bluetooth introduces delay. It's not huge, but if you can see the speaker's face you'll notice voices arriving a beat after the lips move, and once noticed it's hard to un-notice. Wired headphones are meaningfully tighter. I build audio software for a living ([more about that](/about/)), and I keep a pair of wired EarPods in a bag specifically for conversations where I want to watch someone's face while they talk. It makes more difference than any setting.

**Where you put the phone matters more than any of this.** The microphone is at the bottom edge. Phone face-up on the table, bottom edge pointed at the person talking, is a real improvement over phone in your lap. In a café with a hard floor and no soft furnishing, that placement did more for intelligibility than anything I changed in software.

## When none of this is the answer

Amplifying a room makes everything louder, including the espresso machine and the table behind you. In a genuinely loud space, more volume can make speech harder to follow rather than easier, because you're raising the noise along with the voice. Apps with a voice-isolation or focus mode help, because they weight what's in front of you over what isn't. They don't work miracles.

And if you're finding conversations hard often enough to be searching about it, that's worth mentioning to a doctor or audiologist, separately from anything you do with your phone. A phone app is a tool for a situation. It isn't an assessment, and it isn't a substitute for one. If your hearing has changed suddenly, that's not a wait-and-see situation. The [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness) treats sudden hearing loss as something to get seen for quickly.

## The short version

Live Listen is built in and costs nothing, and if you own AirPods you already have it, so use it. If you don't, the feature isn't gated behind your iPhone or your iOS version, it's gated behind Apple's headphone lineup, and no amount of settings-hunting gets around that. The way around it is an app that uses the ordinary audio path, and then the headphones you already own are fine.
