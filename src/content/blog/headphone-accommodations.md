Headphone Accommodations is the setting people switch on hoping it will help them hear, and then can't tell whether it did anything. That's because it does exactly half of what the name suggests. It reshapes the sound your iPhone plays into your headphones, and it leaves the sound of the room alone.

Apple's own pages tell you which buttons to press. The page-one forum threads are people asking each other whether it's worth turning on. Nobody says what it's for, so this does: what it changes, when to leave it on, why your headphones might be quiet at full volume, and what to reach for when the thing you want to hear is a person and not a podcast.

If that last one is you, the shortcut is near the end. AirPods Pro get you part of the way through Transparency mode, and an amplifier app like [Clarive](/) brings the room into whatever headphones you already own. Everything else your iPhone can do for hearing is in our [iPhone hearing tools](/blog/topics/iphone-hearing/) guides.

## What Headphone Accommodations actually does

Think of it as a hearing profile for playback. Two things get adjusted. Soft sounds get lifted, by an amount you choose (Slight, Moderate or Strong), so the quiet parts of a podcast or the far end of a call stop disappearing. And the tone gets tilted: Balanced Tone leaves it alone, Vocal Range pushes the middle frequencies where speech lives, Brightness pushes the top. Apple's [AirPods guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/airpods/set-headphone-accommodations-devcd05671ab/web) lists those as the whole control set, and it is.

You can set them by hand, or you can tap Customize Audio and answer a short listening test. The phone plays a very quiet voice and asks if you heard it, then plays pairs of music clips and asks which sounds better. At the end it proposes a profile. If you have an audiogram in the Health app, from an audiologist or from the [AirPods hearing test](/blog/airpods-hearing-test/), it can build the profile from that instead.

Then you pick where it applies. Phone covers phone calls, FaceTime and third-party video calls. Media covers music, movies, podcasts, audiobooks, Siri, voicemail and, a detail worth knowing, [Live Listen](/blog/live-listen-iphone/). Once it's set on your iPhone the profile follows the headphones, so your iPad, your Mac and your Apple Watch pick it up too.

## What it won't do

Here's the part I wish someone had written down before I spent a week with it on. When I was building Clarive I kept Headphone Accommodations running because it's the closest thing iOS ships to what I was making. It turned out to sit on the other side of the phone from my problem. Everything it does happens to what the phone plays out. The microphone never enters into it.

So a conversation across a table, a lecturer at the front of a hall, a grandchild in the back seat: none of that goes through Headphone Accommodations, because none of it is being played by your phone. The setting can't hear the room.

There's a measurement that makes the point better than I can. When the National Acoustic Laboratories [tested AirPods Pro in noise](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-products/hearing-aids/psap/apple-airpods-pro-for-people-with-hearing-loss-in-noisy-environments), they tried Headphone Accommodations on its own before adding anything else. The signal-to-noise benefit was essentially none. Turning up everything, voice and babble alike, doesn't make the voice easier to pick out. The gains in that study came from [Conversation Boost](/blog/conversation-boost/) and Ambient Noise Reduction, which are directional tricks that live in the AirPods Pro hardware, not from the profile.

The one exception is Transparency mode on AirPods Pro. On the same Headphone Accommodations screen there's a Transparency toggle, and under Customize Transparency you get Amplification, Tone, Ambient Noise Reduction and Own Voice sliders that apply to the outside sound the earbuds pass through. That does bring the room in. But it's the AirPods Pro doing it, and only AirPods Pro.

## Should it be on or off?

On, if any of these is true: calls sound muffled, podcasts make you reach for the volume, or you know your hearing rolls off at the top and speech has lost its crispness. Run Customize Audio in a quiet room rather than guessing at the sliders. The profile it proposes is usually mild, and you can always switch back to the standard settings.

Off, in two cases. If you're happy with how things sound, leave it, because a profile built for speech does change the character of music and not everyone likes it. And if you own AirPods Pro 2 or AirPods Pro 3 and have set up the Hearing Aid feature, this setting is doing nothing: Apple's [support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102663) says Hearing Assistance overrides Headphone Accommodations on iOS 18.1 and later. Use the hearing test and [the Hearing Aid feature](/blog/apple-hearing-aid-feature/) instead. It's the better tool and it replaces this one.

There's no downside to trying it. It doesn't raise the volume ceiling, and switching it off puts everything back.

## How to turn it on and tune it

Connect the headphones first. The screen is there without them, but Customize Audio won't run.

1. Settings, then Accessibility, then Audio & Visual
2. Tap Headphone Accommodations, and turn it on
3. Switch on Phone, Media, or both
4. Tap Customize Audio and follow the prompts, or set Audio Tuning and Level by hand
5. Tap Play Sample to hear the result, and adjust while it plays

Before running Customize Audio, Apple suggests clearing anything that's already coloring the sound: set the Music EQ to Off (Settings, Apps, Music, EQ), center the Balance slider, and if you're on AirPods, set their Equalizer back to Recommended in the Bluetooth settings. Then sit somewhere quiet. A test taken next to a dishwasher gives you a profile for the dishwasher.

## Why your headphones are so quiet at max volume

This is the question that brings people to this screen, and Headphone Accommodations is one of the fixes rather than the first one. Go in this order.

**Headphone Safety.** Settings, Sounds & Haptics, Headphone Safety. Reduce Loud Sounds caps the output at a level you choose, and the cap is the most common reason a pair feels weak. The cap exists for a reason, and the [NIDCD's page on noise-induced hearing loss](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/noise-induced-hearing-loss) explains it plainly: long or repeated exposure to sounds at or above 85 decibels can cause hearing loss. My suggestion is to leave the cap on and lift the other settings first. I only lower mine for a specific quiet recording, and I put it back.

**Balance.** Settings, Accessibility, Audio & Visual, Balance. If it's drifted off center, one side is being turned down and the whole thing feels quiet. Drag it back to the middle. This is also the answer to making one earbud louder than the other, if that's what you actually want.

**Level, in Headphone Accommodations.** Set it to Strong. This is the setting people mean when they say the feature "made everything louder," and it's the right description: it lifts the quiet parts, so the average level goes up without the peaks going anywhere.

**Fit, on AirPods Pro.** A poor seal throws away bass and a surprising amount of loudness. Run the Ear Tip Fit Test in the AirPods settings before you blame software.

If you've done all four and it's still too quiet, the headphones are the limit. Bluetooth earbuds from other brands often have their own volume stage, and some of them simply don't go loud with an iPhone.

## Which headphones it works with

This is the catch that the forum threads miss. Headphone Accommodations only applies to headphones on Apple's list: AirPods 2 and later, AirPods Pro, AirPods Max, wired EarPods with a 3.5 mm plug or Lightning connector, and Beats Fit Pro, Solo Pro, Powerbeats, Powerbeats Fit, Powerbeats Pro and Powerbeats Pro 2. The toggle may be visible with other headphones connected. It won't be doing anything.

Wired EarPods being on the list matters. A pair that costs less than lunch gets the full profile. A $200 pair of Bluetooth headphones from another brand gets none of it.

## If you want to hear the room, not the phone

Say it in the honest order. If conversations have been getting harder over months rather than in one loud restaurant, that's a hearing test and an audiologist, and a hearing aid if they recommend one. No iPhone setting is a substitute for that. Sudden hearing loss in one ear is a [medical emergency](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness), not a settings problem.

If you own AirPods Pro, you already have the best free option. Turn on Transparency in the Headphone Accommodations screen, open Customize Transparency, raise Amplification, and turn on Conversation Boost. On AirPods Pro 2 or 3, take the hearing test and use the Hearing Aid feature instead. If you were going to buy earbuds anyway and the money is there, buy AirPods Pro. I build a listening app and I'll still say that.

## The short version

Headphone Accommodations is worth two minutes: turn it on, run Customize Audio, and calls and podcasts will probably sound clearer. Just know what you turned on. It tunes what the phone plays. For the person across the table, you need AirPods Pro in Transparency mode, an amplifier app, or, if it keeps happening, a hearing test.
