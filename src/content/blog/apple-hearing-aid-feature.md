The hearing aid Apple built isn't a device you buy. It's software, and it lives on exactly two products: AirPods Pro 2 and AirPods Pro 3.

Search this term and you'll get reviews, marketing pages, and a Reddit thread arguing about whether the whole thing is oversold. What you mostly won't get is a straight answer to the question underneath the search, which is whether any of it applies to you. Four gates decide that. Almost every page ranking for this term assumes you've already cleared them.

## What Apple actually means by hearing aid

Apple makes earbuds. In September 2024 it shipped a software feature, named the Hearing Aid feature, that lets AirPods Pro work as an over-the-counter hearing aid for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss. The microphones and speakers were already in the case on your desk. What arrived was code, plus permission from a regulator to call it what it's now called.

That's not a technicality. If it were a device, you'd go buy one. Because it's software welded to particular hardware, the real question is whether you qualify, and the answer is written in requirements lists rather than on a price tag.

It also sits alongside two sibling features that people mix up constantly. The Hearing Test is a five-minute pure-tone check you take at home. Hearing Protection clamps loud environments. The Hearing Aid feature is the one that amplifies quiet speech so you can follow it.

All three run on the same two earbuds. None of them run on anything else Apple sells, and none of them run on your phone alone. If you're mapping what your phone can already do without buying anything, the rest of that territory is covered in our [iPhone hearing tools](/blog/topics/iphone-hearing/) guides.

Here's the whole thing on one screen, which is the table Apple's own pages never quite put together:

| Feature | What it does | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing Test | Pure-tone check at home, about five minutes, result stored in Health | AirPods Pro 2 or 3, adult, quiet room |
| Hearing Aid | Amplifies speech and ambient sound, tuned to your test result | AirPods Pro 2 or 3, adult, mild to moderate loss, Transparency mode |
| Hearing Protection | Reduces loud environments while keeping speech usable | AirPods Pro 2 or 3, on by default in most modes |
| Live Listen | iPhone becomes a remote microphone you place near a speaker | AirPods, some Beats, or a Made for iPhone hearing device |
| Live Captions | Turns speech into on-screen text, no amplification | Recent iPhone, no special headphones |

Two things fall out of that table. The first three rows are one hardware purchase wearing three hats. The last two are the only rows a person without AirPods Pro can use at all, and only one of those makes anything louder.

## The four gates you have to clear

Apple lists the requirements. It lists them politely, halfway down a support page, in the order a manual would use rather than the order that matters. Here they are in the order that matters.

**One: the earbuds.** AirPods Pro 2 or AirPods Pro 3, on current firmware, paired with an iPhone or iPad on current software. Not AirPods 4. Not AirPods Max. Not Beats, not Sony, not the wired EarPods in your drawer. This is the gate that stops the largest number of people, and it costs $249 to clear.

**Two: your age.** The feature is for people 18 and older. That's in Apple's own [Hearing Aid feature instructions](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120992) and in the FDA authorization behind it. It isn't a soft recommendation, it's part of what was authorized.

**Three: how much hearing you've lost.** The intended user has *perceived mild to moderate* hearing loss. If your loss is more than moderate, this feature was not designed for you and won't be enough, which is a thing worth knowing before you spend the money rather than after.

**Four: which noise mode you're in.** Amplification only runs in Transparency. Turn on Noise Cancellation or Adaptive and it stops. I've watched more than one person conclude the feature was broken when they'd simply squeezed the stem and switched modes without noticing.

Region used to be a fifth gate and mostly isn't anymore. These are regulated health features, so each country has to authorize them separately, and Apple's [feature availability page](https://www.apple.com/airpods-pro/feature-availability/) now lists more than 100 countries and regions. Worth a ten-second check if you're outside the US, and no longer the obstacle it was in early 2025.

## What the FDA authorization does and doesn't say

This is the part that got flattened in the coverage, and it's the part that should carry the most weight with you.

On September 12, 2024 the FDA [authorized the first over-the-counter hearing aid software](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-authorizes-first-over-counter-hearing-aid-software). Apple's feature went through the De Novo pathway, which is what regulators use for things that are new and carry low to moderate risk.

It was evaluated in a clinical study of 118 people with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss, across several US sites. The result: people who set it up themselves got about the same perceived benefit as people who had the same device fitted for them by a professional.

Read that carefully, because it's a narrower and more interesting claim than "AirPods are hearing aids now." It says self-fitting works about as well as professional fitting **of this same device**. It says nothing about how this device compares to a fitted hearing aid, and it applies only inside the mild-to-moderate band.

Apple's marketing calls the result "clinical-grade." That's Apple's phrase and I'd treat it as marketing. The authorization is real and it's genuinely a first. It's also a permission to sell, which isn't the same as a measurement of how well the thing works.

## How well it works once you're past the gates

Better than you'd expect from earbuds. Worse than the phrase "hearing aid" makes you picture.

The most useful independent read is audiologist Cliff Olson's [detailed OTC review](https://hearingup.com/blog/apple-airpods-pro-2-detailed-otc-hearing-aid-review), which put the AirPods Pro 2 through real-ear measurement and third-party lab scoring. The numbers are sobering in a specific way.

Usable gain lands around 10 dB, roughly half what a fitted device delivers, and noise reduction tops out near 11 dB.

Untuned, the speech-in-noise score was 0.8 out of 5. Professionally tuned, it climbed to 2.6, which is better and still not good.

Battery is the other thing nobody mentions in the launch coverage. Five to six hours per charge is fine for a dinner and useless as all-day wear, where 12 to 13 hours is the bar. These are earbuds that do a hearing job, not hearing devices that happen to play music.

So the honest summary: in a quiet living room, across a dinner table, on a walk with one other person, it does real work and people are glad they have it. In a restaurant with a tile floor and forty other conversations, the gap between this and a properly fitted device opens wide. That's exactly the situation people most want help with, and it's exactly where the earbuds have least to give.

## Turning it on without fighting it

Setup takes about five minutes and the order matters.

Take the Hearing Test first, on the same AirPods, in a quiet room. The feature tunes itself from that result, and skipping it leaves you adjusting a generic curve by hand. If you already have an audiogram from a clinic, you can add it to the Health app instead and the feature will use that, which is the better input of the two.

Then open your AirPods settings on the iPhone, turn on Hearing Assistance, and set amplification and tone there. Once it's running, the Hearing control in Control Center is where you'll actually live: amplification level, balance, and Conversation Boost, which narrows focus to whoever is directly in front of you.

One habit will save you a lot of confusion. Leave the earbuds in Transparency and stop switching modes. If the amplification vanishes, that's the first thing to check, before firmware, before settings, before anything else.

## What's left on an iPhone if you don't own AirPods Pro

For a lot of people reading this, that's the actual situation, and Apple's answer is thin.

Live Listen, the feature that turns your iPhone into a remote microphone, has the same shape of restriction: it only sends audio to AirPods, some Beats models, and Made for iPhone hearing devices. Generic Bluetooth earbuds and wired headphones can't receive it. We went through that in detail in [Live Listen without AirPods](/blog/live-listen-without-airpods/), and the short version is that no setting unlocks it.

Everything else on the phone is adjacent rather than useful for this. Headphone Accommodations shapes media playback, not the room around you. Background Sounds are for masking. Live Captions read speech as text, which helps in a different way and doesn't make anything louder.

That leaves third-party apps, which sit outside Apple's hardware restrictions because they use the ordinary audio output path every app uses. Any headphones that connect to your iPhone will work with them.

One thing I learned the hard way building in this space: latency matters far more than gain does, and nobody warns you about it. Bluetooth adds enough delay that if you can see the speaker's face, the voice lands a beat after the lips move, and your brain fights the mismatch all evening. Wired headphones through an adapter are noticeably tighter. It's an unglamorous fix and it's the one I'd try first if amplification technically works for you but somehow feels wrong to use.

The second thing, also unglamorous: seal. A loose earbud leaks the amplified sound straight back out and lets the raw room straight in, and no amount of gain fixes it. People chase settings when they should be chasing a tip that fits. That's true of Apple's feature and of every app that does this job.

## Using the headphones you already own

Clarive is the app I build, so treat this as disclosure rather than a neutral recommendation.

Clarive is a sound amplification app for iPhone. The phone's built-in microphone picks up the room, amplification is applied, and the result plays through whatever headphones you have connected. Wired EarPods, ordinary Bluetooth earbuds, over-ear headphones, all fine. No AirPods requirement, no hardware to buy.

Beyond the amplification there are live captions, built on Apple's on-device speech recognition, covering 40+ languages with automatic language detection. Transcripts can be saved and reopened later. Five environment presets (Clear, Conversation, Noisy Place, TV & Media, and Speech Clarity) sit over a Live EQ you can shape by hand, and Voice Isolation narrows things to whoever is in front of you.

All processing happens on the device. No account, no audio sent to a server, and amplification works fully offline. The app is free to download with a limited free tier capped at 5 uses per day, and paid plans range from $4.99 to $29.99 depending on billing period, including a lifetime option. It requires iOS 26 or later, so an older iPhone won't run it.

Where it doesn't compete: Clarive has no hearing test, so it can't tune itself to your audiogram the way Apple's feature does. That's a real advantage Apple has and I'm not going to talk around it. If you own AirPods Pro 2 or 3 and your loss is mild to moderate, Apple's Hearing Aid feature is fitted to you in a way a general-purpose app can't be, and it's the one to use.

## So should you buy the earbuds

If you already own AirPods Pro 2 or 3, turn the feature on today. It costs nothing, it's genuinely well built, and the setup is fifteen minutes at worst.

If you don't own them and you're weighing $249 against the alternatives, the calculus is less obvious than the coverage suggested. For occasional help in reasonable rooms, they're a good buy, and you get a decent pair of earbuds out of it either way. Daily all-waking-hours wear is a different story, and the battery alone rules them out. Loud rooms, which is what sends people looking in the first place, are where they help least.

And if what you're really trying to work out is whether you should be seeing someone about your hearing, the NIDCD notes that [only about one in five people who'd benefit from a hearing aid uses one](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/hearing-aids). Earbuds are not why. The gap is mostly cost, access, and how long people wait. If conversations have been getting harder for a while, an appointment will tell you more than any product page will, and it's worth making before you spend anything. If your hearing dropped suddenly, in one ear or both, that's urgent and it needs a doctor now rather than an app.

Anything you buy in the meantime is a tool for tonight's dinner. Worth having. Not the answer to the larger question. You can read more about who's behind this site on the [about page](/about/).
